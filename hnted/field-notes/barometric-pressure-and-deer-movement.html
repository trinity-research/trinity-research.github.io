Does barometric pressure predict deer movement?

Trinity Research LLC · Published November 1, 2026 · 2 min read

I think pressure is worth watching. What I want to know is whether it helps us make a better hunting decision, rather than just giving us another number to look at.

What does the research tell us?

Pressure changes along with wind, temperature, and rain. Mississippi's wildlife agency points out that it is hard to separate those effects. Their summary does not give us one pressure reading that reliably means bucks will be moving in daylight. [1]

An Oklahoma GPS study found weather relationships that changed with the season, the hour, and whether the deer were bucks or does. Pressure showed a relationship in a few comparisons. That makes me interested in the pattern, but I would be careful about building a whole hunt around a particular reading. [2]

How I would use it

For HNTED, I want to see where pressure is now and what it has done over the last three hours. An upward bar means rising pressure. A downward bar means falling pressure. I still want the wind, temperature change, and rain beside it so I can see the whole situation.

Say the pressure is falling before an afternoon change. I would look at when the wind is expected to turn and whether that changes the stand I can use. That is already useful information, even before we know whether the pressure trend adds anything to a movement prediction.

Let the cameras help answer it

This is where I think deer cameras could help. We could compare separate visits with the weather at the time the pictures were taken. A burst of ten pictures of one deer should not count as ten different visits. We would also need to know when the camera was actually working.

I want to keep the quiet mornings in that record too. Otherwise we will mostly remember the days that worked. After enough similar days, we may find a pattern on that ground. Until then, I would use pressure as another piece of the picture and keep watching what the deer actually do.

Sources & scope

  2. Webb et al. (2010): Measuring Fine-Scale White-Tailed Deer MovementsPeer-reviewed GPS study; Oklahoma
